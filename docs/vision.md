# Product vision

## One-sentence pitch

Pi Constellation Mapper is a pocketable night-sky companion that lets people understand the real stars above them and turn those stars into constellations and stories of their own.

## Why build it?

Most sky-map apps are excellent reference tools but live inside general-purpose phones and emphasize lookup. This project explores a more focused object: something made specifically for being outside at night, learning the sky, and making meaning from it.

The creative half matters as much as the astronomical half. A custom constellation should feel like a small artifact worth keeping and sharing, not a temporary drawing layered over a map.

## Who it is for

- Curious stargazers who want a dedicated, distraction-free tool
- Families and educators telling stories through the night sky
- Makers interested in astronomy, embedded interfaces, and portable hardware
- Artists who want to work with real stellar positions as a creative canvas

## What success looks like

A person can take the device outside, see a recognizable view of the sky, identify a few stars, draw a new constellation, give it a name and story, and find that creation again later—all without needing a network connection.

## Scope boundaries for the first version

The first version is not trying to be:

- a telescope controller
- an astrophotography system
- a precision navigation instrument
- a complete replacement for mature planetarium software
- dependent on every possible sensor before the basic interaction works

The first version proves the loop: **orient, explore, connect, name, save**.

## Working product decisions

- Begin with a desktop-compatible prototype before committing to hardware.
- Treat custom constellations as portable data, not screenshots.
- Keep the core useful offline.
- Design for dark outdoor use from the beginning.
- Add sensors only when they remove meaningful friction.

## Questions for the first prototype

1. Is selecting and connecting stars satisfying on a small screen?
2. What information helps recognition without cluttering the sky?
3. How quickly can a user move between observing and creating?
4. Which physical control is most useful with gloves or cold hands?
5. What is the minimum star catalog that still feels like the real sky?
